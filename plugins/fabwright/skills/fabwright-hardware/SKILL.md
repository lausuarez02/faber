# Building a real device with Fabwright

Fabwright is a hardware sandbox with a real simulator, reached through the `fabwright` MCP tools (or the `fabwright` CLI with the same operations). A person who wants a device wants to **hold it**: so from the first answer, think about what they will buy, what they will print and how they will put the stuff inside. Every error from Fabwright says what failed, why and the fix: do what it says.

## The loop

1. **Goal.** Say back what the device does in one sentence in the person's words. If the wish is ambiguous (or they changed their mind mid-sentence: take the last version), ask ONE question and wait.
2. **Plan.** `plan_device` {goal, constraints} first (pocket: true for anything carried), then tell the person the plan in plain words: what it does, the board and parts and why, size, battery life, price, and what is simulated. One project per idea: `new_project` {"folder": true, "name": …}.
3. **Smallest parts that fit.** Choose the smallest parts that do the job (`search_parts`, `get_part`), place and wire them (`place_part`, `connect`), `check_design` until it is clean. Before any case: `check_fit` with the shape and size the person wants, then `propose_layout`; read costs and relax what is costly.
4. **Think assembly.** Think how it goes together before the case exists, using `propose_layout`'s placements and layers: what mounts where, the insertion order, the wire room, access to USB / buttons / switch, battery safety (the checklist below). If a part cannot go in, change the layout or the part now.
5. **Design the case.** `design_case` with the chosen layout (any shape: a preset or "custom" with an outline you draw), show the renders and the measured size and checks (pocket verdict, screen window, button travel, USB access). `update_case` to change it.
6. **How it goes together.** `get_assembly_plan` on the designed case: which shell each part goes into and what holds it (the case adds seat posts, hold-downs, backstops and cell corners), the insertion order, when each wire is soldered, the battery last, a test before closing, a picture per step. Fix what its assembly-* checks flag (`update_case`, another layout or a part) before printing, and tell the person the order in a few steps.
7. **Firmware and tests.** `write_file` the firmware, `run` it, `write_scenario` for each behaviour of the goal and `run_tests` until they pass; `fidelity_report` for what the simulation cannot check.
8. **The build kit.** `get_build_kit`: everything to buy, print, assemble and flash, with totals; `export_manufacturing` when the person wants the files for a supplier or a printer.

## Rules of thumb

- Pocket and hand-held devices (a keychain, a pet, a remote, an egg): aim for 20 mm thick or less. Thickness is what makes a pocket device feel wrong; width and length matter less.
- Pick the smallest parts that do the job, then prove they fit: `check_fit` (under a second, nothing written) says what misses by how many mm and which smaller parts would fit (a smaller cell, a smaller screen, a board on its edge). Tell the person the trade-off (a smaller cell means shorter battery life) and let them choose.
- Battery devices: prefer a board with a built-in LiPo charger and USB-C: `search_parts` {"query": "charger", "category": "board"} lists them first. One board instead of board + charger module is fewer wires, less room, one USB port and one fewer thing to assemble. If `plan_device` picked a board without one, say so and offer the swap (the board's language and pins may change).
- Do not pin positions (placements x_mm / y_mm / layer / face) unless the person asked for that spot: Fabwright's layout is usually smaller. Pinned parts show up in costs with what they add.
- Leave dome_mm and the edges on auto: each mm of dome is a mm of thickness on each face.
- Read costs in every `check_fit` / `propose_layout` / `design_case` / `update_case` answer (what each of your choices adds, in mm and cm³, with the call that relaxes it) and relax the costly ones unless the person asked for them.
- Call `propose_layout` before `design_case`: it shows the thinnest, smallest and balanced ways to put the parts inside, with the reasons. Tell the person which one and why; layout: "<id>" picks it.
- Be honest about what was not simulated: `fidelity_report` and plan_device's cannot_check list it (real feel, sound, battery life, stand-ins). The print, the fit of real parts and the hand feel were not tested: say so, and say what to check on the first real unit.
- Never put API keys, passwords or tokens in firmware or in fabwright.json: `set_network` takes secrets by NAME and the values live in the project's .env. Firmware that talks to a service calls it at its real host through the relay (FabwrightRelay.h, fabwrightRelayUrl): the relay is generated from fabwright.json and holds the keys, so there is no backend of your own to invent.
- Test behaviour that happens over time, at random or offline as scenarios, not by hand: each test seeds the board's random numbers from its id (esp_random / random() repeat; `write_scenario` "seed" tries another sequence, `run` {"seed"} live), so "at a random moment" is repeatable. Check order and timing with "preceded_by" ({"expect_capture": "cam", "preceded_by": {"said": "picture in 10", "min_ms": 9000, "max_ms": 11000}}); "said" also matches the text firmware sends to a speech API; check uploads by what they carry ({"request": {"host", "method", "path", "content_type", "body_json"}}); go offline and back with {"network_fault": {"kind": "wifi_drop"}} / "clear", and reboot with {"reset_board": true} (flash files survive) to prove "it posts later". Mock any API from its OpenAPI file (`add_mock_api` {"kind": "openapi", "spec": "network/api.yaml"}): requests that do not fit the spec get 400 with why. A service you run on this computer is reachable only when its host opts in (`set_network` allow {"host", "local": true, "to": "http://127.0.0.1:<port>"}).
- A device with a camera shows when it is about to take, and is taking, a picture: an LED or the screen while it captures (check_design camera-indicator warns otherwise), ideally a spoken or on-screen countdown first with a way to cancel it, and photos kept on the device until their owner chooses to share them (review before publishing, no location data). Say this to the person when you design one; a camera must never be concealed.
- For a battery device, ask (or assume and say) how long it should run, then `estimate_power`: battery life from each load's datasheet current and duty cycle (camera always on vs per picture, Wi-Fi connected vs bursts, the speaker, sleep). Save the target and the real pattern with `set_power` {"target_hours", "duty"}; check_design then warns (battery-life) under the target with the cell that would make it. It is an estimate: say so.
- When the case holds something that is not electronics (a tin of pouches or mints, a lip balm, a lighter, a card deck), declare it on the case, never as a fake part: `design_case` / `check_fit` {"held": [{"id", "name", "shape": "cylinder" | "box" | "custom", "size_mm", "clearance_mm", "insert": "slide" | "pocket" | "snap", "from", "removable", "source", "sensor": {"part"}}]}. Find its real size from more than one source (the maker's page, measurements people posted) and say where it came from in "source"; tell the person to measure their own with calipers (the report's held[].measure and the build kit's "What it holds" say how) and that clearance_mm is the knob if it is tight or loose. The case keeps every part and wire out of its room, cuts its opening and checks the walls round it (held-object) and that a lid sensor sits close enough (held-sensor). Depths through the front / back count from the case's OUTER face: the report's held[].depth_mm and proud_mm say how deep it sits and how far it stands out. When the case rides ON the object (a puck on a can's lid, a dock, a cap) only part of it goes in: a socket, "engage_mm" (how deep, from the outer face) with "fit": "slip" | "press" | "snap", and the case outline can follow the object with "follow": {"held": "<id>", "margin_mm"} (a round puck the can's size). A magnet glued on the object, outside the case, is "magnet": {"beyond_mm", "angle_deg"}: held-sensor then gives the gap mounted and lifted off (sensor.lift_mm) and the magnet_mm values to drive the switch in a test.
- A device that shows something all day on a small battery: an e-paper screen (epaper-154-ssd1681: the image stays with no power; refresh about 2 s full, 0.5 s partial, ghosts unless the previous image is kept) or a Sharp memory LCD (sharp-memory-lcd-128: a microamp for a still image, VCOM toggled each second), never a TFT / OLED that must stay lit. The board deep-sleeps between events (esp_deep_sleep_start with a timer and a GPIO wake-up from a reed / Hall switch or a button): the simulation runs no firmware while it sleeps, so a test step can let hours pass in seconds, and RTC_DATA_ATTR variables, the clock and esp_sleep_get_wakeup_cause() survive like on the chip. Day counters: an RTC part (a scenario's "clock" and its clock steps set it too, so a test crosses midnight) or NTP; for a battery device pick a small one without a power LED: ds3231-mini-rtc (same RTClib code, 14 x 14 mm, 0.11 mA) or rv3028-rtc (19 x 19 x 4 mm, 45 nA), not the ZS-042 ds3231-rtc, whose LED draws about 2 mA (desolder it and its charging diode, prop powerLed false, if you keep it). Thin cells: 401730 / 402030 are 4 mm, 502030 / 503035 5 mm. Then `estimate_power` with power.wakes_per_day and awake_s (or after a run: the measured wake pattern) for battery life in days; say it is an estimate.

## Always think how it goes inside

Before the case exists, and again when the person asks how to build it:

- **Where each part mounts.** For every part, say what holds it: the screen sits behind its window on ledges, the board on standoffs or in a pocket, the cell in its own pocket, the speaker against its grille, buttons under their plungers, a sensor at its window or air holes. A part nothing holds rattles or breaks a joint.
- **Insertion order.** Write the order a person puts it together, front shell face down: window part (screen) first, then buttons / plungers / light pipes, then the board, then the wires, the battery last, then close. If a part cannot go in after the one before it, change the layout (layers) now, not after printing.
- **Wire room.** Hand-wired devices need room for solder joints and bends (about 3 to 5 mm above pins, more where many wires meet) and a short service loop so the halves open without tearing a joint. Route wires away from the closing seam and screw bosses. Battery devices are built soldered: `set_assembly` "soldered" so the pack has wire cuts and soldering steps.
- **Access from outside.** The USB-C port must reach its wall with room for the plug's body (charging and flashing), every button must line up with its plunger, a power switch must be reachable, and a screen must face its window. Boards that need BOOT / RESET to flash: flash before closing, or leave a pin hole.
- **Battery safety.** Use a protected LiPo cell (with its protection circuit) on a connector so it can be unplugged, charged only by a charger (the board's built-in one or a charger module), with the switch after the charger. Nothing presses on the cell: no screw, boss or sharp edge over it, a little room for swelling, and keep it away from the regulator's heat. Never solder onto the cell's tabs.
- **Closing and opening.** Choose how the halves close (snap clips or screws; the case answer says which and where) and whether the person must open it again (a cell that is not charged over USB, an SD card). Heavy parts low, the cell flat.
- **Printing it.** Each body is written in its print orientation with PRINT.md (material, layer height, supports, tolerances). Suggest a test print of the front half first to check the screen and buttons with the real parts; tolerances differ per printer.

## What do I need to build it?

Call `get_build_kit` (after `design_case`; it says what is missing when there is no case yet) and answer in four short parts, with totals:

- **Buy:** each part with quantity, price and where, plus wire, heat-shrink and screws; the total price. Name the real part when a stand-in was simulated.
- **Print:** each printed body (front, back, plungers, light pipes) with the material, layer height and supports from PRINT.md, and print time or grams when given; where the STL / 3MF files are.
- **Assemble:** the numbered steps in insertion order (power path first when soldered), the tools needed (soldering iron, wire strippers, screwdriver) and the checks before closing.
- **Flash:** how the firmware gets on the board (the file and one line on how), and the quick test once it is closed.

Then one line on what was not tested (the print, the real fit, the hand feel, real battery life) and offer the pack for a supplier or a printer.

## Talking to the person

- Many people are beginners: say "the screen", not "SSD1306 over I2C"; answer in their language.
- Show pictures (renders, screenshots) and give links and file paths, not terminal commands, unless they asked for one.
- Short answers: the plan, the trade-off to choose, then the result.
